I have not been able to put down the question in your last letter: is the concentration of energy we keep tracking really heading toward a singularity, or is it something our truncation manufactures?

Here is where I stand. Leray's global weak solutions exist, of course, but in three dimensions their uniqueness and smoothness are still open. Caffarelli, Kohn and Nirenberg tell us only that the singular set has zero one-dimensional parabolic Hausdorff measure, which does not rule it out. Nečas, Růžička and Šverák excluded Leray's self-similar blow-up in L³, yet asymptotically self-similar scenarios remain on the table. On the Euler side Onsager's conjecture is settled, the conservation half by Constantin, E and Titi and the dissipative half by Isett's convex integration, and Buckmaster and Vicol have shown that in a class weaker than Leray–Hopf, Navier–Stokes weak solutions need not be unique. How much pathology the weak theory admits depends on how far we let the function space go. Tao's averaged equation, which does blow up, is a standing warning: no argument built only on the energy identity and that kind of harmonic-analytic structure can prove global regularity.

So I would like to ask differently. Rather than proving that nothing ever blows up, let us record exactly how energy moves between scales: at which scales and times it piles up abnormally, along which structures, and how much of each pile-up belongs to the equation rather than to discretisation and cut-offs. What can be proved, we prove, with estimates. What cannot, we pin down by numerical experiment within a range someone else can check, and we call it a conjecture and go no further.

The precise set-up, the cases we have already excluded and what this stage should deliver are in the shared material; please take them from there. I will not repeat them in a letter. A paraphrase drifts, and I would rather you read the source before you read my bias.

I look forward to your reply.
