Mathematical physics research session · opening note (the continuation of a long-running project)

This is a problem in mathematical physics that we have been circling for a long time. It touches the theory of phase transitions in lattice systems with long-range hard constraints; renormalization-group flow, and the question of which few operators are relevant and which are washed out under coarse-graining; cluster expansions and the radius beyond which they stop converging; Lee–Yang zeros creeping toward the real axis as the system grows; the rugged energy landscapes of glassy systems, where replica symmetry breaks and the system ages instead of equilibrating; and, more recently, Wasserstein gradient flows and entropy production far from equilibrium, which give the cleanest language we have for "where the effort actually goes".

The system itself is finite and, on paper, unremarkable: many interacting degrees of freedom with simple local rules. In practice it shows every sign of a hidden critical point. Order parameters jump where no local quantity predicts it. Correlation lengths grow until they span the whole system. Small changes to the rules move the entire phase diagram. The aim is to identify the relevant directions, compute how they flow, draw the phase diagram precisely enough to act on, and locate the operating point that is optimal under fixed resource budgets.

We work in two modes at once. One is rigorous: bounds, constructions, counterexamples, and proofs that a property holds throughout a phase, or the exact configuration where it fails. The other is experimental: a statement is accepted only after it has been measured on the real system, and estimates without measurements are recorded as conjectures together with the measurement that would decide them.

The complete statement — definitions, constraints that are settled and not to be reopened, the partial results of earlier sessions, and the exact deliverable format — is in the shared material, not in this message. I am deliberately not summarising it: a summary loses precision, and it would bias the analysis before you have read the source.
