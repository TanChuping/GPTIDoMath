**On the present state of a long-running problem**

Since Poincaré's work on the three-body problem it has been known that the long-term behaviour of a deterministic system can be too intricate for any explicit formula. KAM theory, in the middle of the twentieth century, supplied the other half of the picture: for nearly integrable Hamiltonian systems most invariant tori survive small perturbations, and on most of phase space the motion stays quasi-periodic. Arnold soon gave examples showing that with three or more degrees of freedom a slow, long-range drift can still occur through the gaps between the tori. How typical this Arnold diffusion is, and how fast it proceeds, is still partly unsettled.

A second thread runs through hyperbolicity. Uniformly hyperbolic systems are well understood, and Pesin theory carries much of that understanding over to the non-uniform case; Palis's conjectured global picture of typical dynamics, however, remains open. Several landmarks of the field were computer-assisted: Lanford's proof of the Feigenbaum renormalisation fixed point, and Tucker's proof that the Lorenz attractor exists, which answered Smale's fourteenth problem. They model a way of working we mean to follow, in which rigorous interval bounds and genuine computation check each other and neither is optional.

The system we care about is finite-dimensional, and its rules look mild locally. Over long times, though, it drifts, lingers and switches abruptly in ways that no single local estimate explains. Our present reading is that what matters may be a few channels visible only in the full phase space, together with the slow variables that govern them. The aim of this stage is to locate those channels and variables, to prove what can be proved, to give checkable computational evidence for the rest, and to draw the line between the two explicitly.

This note explains only where the problem comes from and how we think about it. The system itself, the constraints already fixed, the earlier results and the form this delivery should take are in the shared material, which takes precedence; restating them here would only lose precision.
