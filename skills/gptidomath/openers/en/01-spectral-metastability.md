Mathematical research session · opening note (the continuation of a long-running project)

This is a mathematics problem, and one we have been chasing for a long time without fully taming it. It sits where several fields meet: the spectral theory of non-reversible Markov operators on enormous finite state graphs; metastability, and the Eyring–Kramers picture of rare transitions between deep wells; free probability and the edge statistics of large structured random matrices; and isoperimetric inequalities of Cheeger type, which tie a spectral gap to a geometric bottleneck. Underneath sits a question we keep coming back to: are the slow modes we observe genuine invariants of the dynamics, or artefacts of how the state space was coarse-grained? Would some hidden symmetry quotient collapse the spectrum into something we could actually prove?

The object looks ordinary: a finite system of many interacting components, each simple on its own, whose joint evolution is anything but. Inside that ordinariness we keep finding quasi-stationary states that live far longer than any local estimate allows, bottlenecks that appear only at full scale, and perturbations that are harmless one at a time yet shift the whole spectrum when they act together. The aim is to describe the reachable structure and its slow modes precisely, to isolate the few bottlenecks that really govern the behaviour, and to approach the frontier where cost and benefit trade off optimally.

The method is half proof and half experiment. On the proof side we construct counterexamples, bound operators, and either prove that a property holds on every reachable state or exhibit the exact trajectory that breaks it. On the experimental side, a claim counts only after it has been checked against measurements of the real system, and a conjecture stays labelled as one.
